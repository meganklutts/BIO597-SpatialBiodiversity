How do communities change across the landscape?

Well, how do we quantify community change? Richness is one dimension. Community composition another.

A richness map can remain constant while community composition changes completely.

Ask students to count species in every pond, then ask which pair of ponds would be most similar. The same richness of three cannot distinguish ABC from DEF. Composition retains identities. Occurrence counts are useful for recording the data, but the Jaccard comparisons today use presence/absence. A color used for composition is a location in a multivariate summary, rather than a scale from low to high biodiversity. The species letters are invented labels; the animal symbols are schematic amphibians.
From observations to communities

The following slides distinguish species richness from composition and then introduce a spatial grid as one explicit, repeatable way to define comparable sites.
If we have spatially continuous samples, how do we determine 'communities'?

The same points can form different communities

A simple way is to partition the landscape into grid cells with a given grain (grid cell size)

The size of the gridcell will determine community composition.

The physical extent and every point are unchanged. Only the grain changes. In the lab, 10 km cells nest within 20 km cells, which nest within 40 km cells because the origin is fixed. Projected coordinates let the cell widths have meter units. A larger cell combines records from locations that might represent different ponds, elevations or habitats. A grid is a practical definition of a sampled community, not a claim that every species interacts uniformly throughout that square. Wiens develops the distinction between grain and extent and the ecological consequences of scale.
What aggregation does

A larger cell pool more species

Increasing grain may (or may not) increase species richness of a grid cell.

This may (or may not) have consequences for downstream analysis, and this is one of the things we will explore in the lab today.

Count each small list: two, two, two and one species. Their mean is 1.75, while the union contains four distinct species. Neither summing richness to seven nor averaging to 1.75 gives the richness of the pooled cell. Counts of repeated species are combined in the community matrix; presence/absence is then calculated from the pooled counts. Some small cells could have no records. Pooling does not prove that every species occupies every habitat within the larger square. Ask whether the ecological meaning of a site changes even if the underlying animals do not.

Quantifying community difference

Two patterns can determine community difference

Jaccard dissimilarity (difference in community composition) can include replacement and nestedness.

Nestedness means one community is a subset of another

Count the unions and shared species in both comparisons. AB versus ABCD has two shared of four, while ABC versus ACD also has two shared of four. The same total difference therefore does not establish the same biological process. A subset might represent environmental filtering, local loss, or incomplete detection; a replacement pattern might reflect sorting or dispersal history. Lab11 calculates total Jaccard, not a formal partition of its components. Avoid labeling every high-Jaccard cell as high species replacement. This is a repeated toy figure from Lecture10 so students can connect the metric to its spatial use.
Pairwise dissimilarity for all communities

Each comparison needs two species lists

Keep site order aligned when pairing species differences with geographic distances.

Worked example · Jaccard = 1 − shared / union; schematic distances, not to scale.

AB shares A and B out of four species in its union, giving dissimilarity 0.5. BC shares D out of five, giving 0.8. AC shares none and has dissimilarity one. The three geographic distances form a 30–40–50 triangle and are illustrative, not Maine measurements. Similarity is one minus dissimilarity. The matrix and coordinates must use the same site ordering; otherwise the response for one pair could be attached to the predictors of another. If sites without climate are removed later, the pairs must be calculated again.
Distance decay of similarity

Do distant sites share fewer recorded species?

Distance decay summarizes a pattern. Its strength and shape can change with grain.

Original Maine data · points = pairs; lines = means in 50 km distance bands.

The left panel shows every 20 km pair and the mean in each 50 km band. The right panel compares the same band procedure among grains. Descriptive distance–similarity correlations are -0.165, -0.199, and -0.322. The negative association is broad rather than a rule for every pair. Check pair counts before interpreting the longest-distance bins: they involve fewer possible site combinations and particular corners of Maine. Coarser grids pool species and change the site and pair distributions. The line is a descriptive average, not a fitted GDM or a confidence interval.
Community change over the landscape

Several processes can produce distance decay

Environment, dispersal, history, and observation can act together.

In the first cartoon, increasingly different climates can favor different species. In the second, similar-looking habitats can support different communities because of a barrier or different colonization histories. In the third, fewer observations produce a shorter recorded list even if the underlying list is the same. These are possible explanations, not mechanisms estimated separately by the cartoon or established from a scatterplot. Ask what extra information would distinguish them: habitat measurements, repeated detection surveys, movement data, historical context, or independent site samples.
Neighborhood similarity

Which recorded communities differ nearby?

Mean neighbor Jaccard describes local compositional difference.

Worked example · same-radius comparisons are made within each grain.

Walk through the seven comparisons rather than beginning with an array operation. Three neighbors match ABC exactly, contributing zero. Four contain only DEF and contribute one. The gray neighbor is not a sampled empty community and is excluded. The mean is four sevenths, approximately 0.57. The lab includes sampled cell centers within 1.5 cell widths, which includes diagonal neighbors. That threshold is 15, 30 or 60 km as grain increases. This is not a replacement-only measure, nor is it divided by geographic distance. A focal cell with no sampled neighbors receives a missing value.
Richness and difference answer different questions

A rich site need not be the most distinctive

Use the species lists and record totals to interpret a high local difference.

Original Maine data · gray indicates no mapped value, not zero difference.

The maps use the same sampled 20 km cells but different metrics and color scales. High richness asks how many species were recorded in a cell. High neighbor dissimilarity asks how different the recorded list is from nearby lists. A species-poor cell can appear highly different because its list is a subset or because it was poorly sampled. High local difference is therefore not automatically a conservation priority or proof of a transition zone. Ask students to nominate a cell they would inspect in the matrix before explaining it biologically.
Add environmental context

A site has an environmental setting

All 19 bioclimatic variables enter GDM; these two maps orient us to the landscape.

Original Maine data · WorldClim variable definitions.

Each climate value averages valid raster pixels intersecting the cell land area, rather than extracting climate only where records happen to be concentrated. Edge pixels are included equally, so this is an approximate zonal mean. BIO1 and BIO12 are displayed as recognizable temperature and precipitation examples; the fitted full models also use seasonality, extremes and quarterly summaries. Many of the 19 variables are correlated because they derive from the same monthly climate. WorldClim 2.1 summarizes 1970–2000, while the records span multiple years. Climate does not directly measure wetland hydroperiod, canopy, disease or detectability.
Generalized dissimilarity modeling

Relate community differences to site differences

GDM predicts the expected difference between communities.

Illustrated inputs · method: Ferrier et al. (2007).

We have one climate vector per site and one observed dissimilarity per pair. GDM uses environmental differences and optionally geographic separation to model the expected community difference. It does not produce a presence probability for a specific amphibian or a richness prediction. The numeric climate values are illustrative; two variables are written out to keep the figure readable, but the core lab uses all 19. The geographic term uses straight-line projected distance, not a measured route or dispersal mechanism. Compare geography alone with geography plus climate, using the same retained sites in both models.
The main nonlinear intuition

Equal climate steps need not mean equal change

GDM stretches parts of a climate gradient where communities change more.

Illustrated transformation · this curve is not a fitted lab result.

Both temperature intervals are two degrees wide. In the flat lower segment, their transformed values remain close. In the steep segment, the same temperature step has a much larger transformed difference. Fitted smooth increasing transformations allow this rate to vary along the gradient. The curve is invented to explain the operation, not a fitted Maine threshold or an amphibian physiological limit. An increasing cumulative transformation is not a claim that richness increases with temperature. Its difference between the two site values contributes to predicted dissimilarity.
Environmental correlates of community turnover

Steep segments identify intervals of modeled change

Read the slope and climate interval, then compare grains.

These are fitted Maine curves, unlike the earlier stretching cartoon. The plotted predictors are selected by their 20 km transformation heights; the full model still uses all 19. The x axis stays in the original WorldClim scale for that predictor; the y axis is partial ecological distance. The helper varies one climate variable, holds other inputs fixed, and subtracts the starting transformed value. Steep sections indicate more fitted community change within a particular interval. Projecting estimated community turnover across the landscape

Summarize the transformed environments

Transform first, then use PCA to display the major axes of expected compositional variation.

Method: PCA · implementation: gdmbayes.

The package supplies several weighted spline columns for each environmental predictor. Sum them within each predictor to obtain 19 transformed columns, one row per modeled site. These transformed values already contain the fitted GDM weights. PCA is centered but we do not standardize every column to unit variance, because doing so would give a very weak transformed predictor the same variance as a strong one. Retain two components for the plot, report their explained variance, and recognize that remaining components are omitted. The geographic term is pairwise and does not form a site-level transformation in this map.
One color key in two spaces

Nearby colors summarize similar expected composition

PC1 + PC2 retain 91.7% of the GDM-transformed variation.

Original Maine data · first two PCs; geographic-distance effects are excluded from the site colors.

Red and green encode separately rescaled PC1 and PC2 scores; blue is held constant. The very same site has the very same color in map and scatterplot. The components explain 65.2% and 26.5%, leaving 8.3% outside the display. Similar colors indicate similar values in this two-axis summary of expected composition associated with climate; they do not establish identical species lists or calibrated Jaccard distances. Colors are not a scale from poor to rich biodiversity. Gray areas have no modeled site. A separate model or grain gets its own PCA orientation and color scale, so matching colors across runs are not a common biological legend.
Connect the ordination to environmental biology

Which gradients align with the displayed change?

Two longest arrows: BIO5 and BIO17.

Original Maine data · select by length in the PC1–PC2 plane; display only two arrows.

The selected variables are BIO5: max temp warmest and BIO17: precip driest q. Loadings are computed as component directions multiplied by the square root of component variance. Rank the two-dimensional vector lengths, sqrt(PC1² + PC2²), rather than the signed sum, which can cancel contributions or change when an axis flips. Plot only those two arrows to keep the biplot readable. Arrow direction represents increasing transformed predictor value. Their lengths describe contributions in the displayed plane, not total GDM importance over all dimensions. The maps show original climate values so the spatial gradients can be related to the biological setting. An arrow does not demonstrate causation.

Which map would help locate a richness hotspot, a local contrast, or an environmental transition?

Have students answer each question before naming the map. Richness counts recorded species. Neighbor dissimilarity measures differences among observed lists in a defined neighborhood.
