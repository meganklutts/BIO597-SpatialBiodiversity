One decision tree

A question separates groups of examples

Within each final group, predict the average label.

There are, on average, more background points in the 'cold' class when the split is at 5 degrees C.

Illustration only. The model learns its own thresholds.

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Follow one route through the tree. The 5°C threshold and group counts are invented for teaching. A regression tree with default squared-error loss predicts the mean training label in a leaf. The algorithm searches for splits that reduce variation in those labels. Source: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/tree.html. Image generated for this lecture; see artwork notes.
A deeper decision tree

Two levels of temperature splits

A 6°C location follows the highlighted path and receives a score of 0.8.

Depth 2 means two splits along each path.

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Start at the top with a hypothetical location whose annual mean temperature is 6°C. Is it warmer than 5°C? Yes, follow the right branch. Is it warmer than 7°C? No, follow the left branch into the group above 5°C and up to 7°C. That final group has eight presence labels and two background labels, so its mean label is 8/10 = 0.8. Ask students to trace 2°C, 4°C, and 8°C, which end at scores 0.1, 0.3, and 0.4. Depth counts the number of splits from root to final group: the root is depth 0, its child questions are depth 1, and the four final groups are depth 2. The tree uses temperature repeatedly, so one predictor can produce several different scores and a response that rises and then falls. All thresholds, counts, and scores are invented to illustrate the mechanism, not fitted to the wood-frog data. Source: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/tree.html. Diagram generated for this lecture; see assets/Lecture07-artwork-notes.md.

A random forest

Many trees contribute to one score

Each tree learns from a different resample of the training records.

The forest averages their scores.

Three trees shown here. The lab uses 100.

Timing: 1.5 minutes. The scores are illustrative, not fitted results. Resampling means choosing training rows with replacement, so the trees see different combinations of records. Averaging can reduce the sensitivity of one tree to one particular sample; it does not eliminate bias. The lab uses RandomForestRegressor with bootstrap=True and the default max_features=1.0, so it considers all selected features at each split. Other forest settings can also randomize predictor choice. No need to introduce ensemble or bootstrap jargon unless asked. Source: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/ensemble.html#forests-of-randomized-trees.

Average error = (0.2 + 0.3 + 0.6) / 3 ≈ 0.37

Mean absolute error (MAE). Lower is better.

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Work through the first row aloud and invite a student to calculate the last one. Absolute means ignore the sign. We average across all withheld rows. Predicting 0.5 everywhere gives MAE=0.5 for binary labels. This is error against our assigned presence/background labels, not an error in true occupancy probability. Source: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.metrics.mean_absolute_error.html.
Scaling to more predictors

Does adding more features (more predictors) add useful information?

Would two places with similar temperature always suit a frog equally well? What if one was super arid and the other humid?

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Pause before moving on. Ask about moisture, drying, breeding pools and seasonal conditions. BIO1 is annual mean temperature in degrees Celsius, BIO12 is annual precipitation in mm, and BIO15 is precipitation seasonality expressed as coefficient of variation. These are broad climate summaries rather than pond measurements. Source: https://www.worldclim.org/data/bioclim.html. The maps come from the provided Maine NetCDF.

Variable importance

Which variables helped the trees separate labels?

Not all variables contribute equally. Variable importance isn't necessarily _explanatory_, it is correlated though.

Timing: 1 minute. These are the forest’s built-in impurity-based feature_importances_: contribution to reducing training split error, normalized to sum to one. A high bar does not prove an ecological cause, and this is not held-out importance. Correlated predictors can substitute for one another and split credit. Variables with more candidate split values can receive biased importance. Source: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/ensemble.html#feature-importance-evaluation.
